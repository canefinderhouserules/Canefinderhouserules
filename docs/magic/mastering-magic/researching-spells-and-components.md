# Researching Spells, Components, Wishes and Magic Items

Guidance for the GM when the PCs take control of their own rewards: researching and designing spells, tracking spell components, handling wishes, and creating new magic items. See also [Designing Spells](designing-spells.md).

## Research and Designing Spells

Before allowing a PC to introduce a new spell, request a detailed write-up of the spell using the standard spell format. Based on this write-up, determine whether the spell is balanced for its level and appropriate for the game.

**Spell Categories**: Spells divide into offensive magic (direct damage, enhancing combat abilities, or summoning allies), defensive magic (protecting the caster or allies, controlling or impeding enemies, or healing), and utility magic (travel magic and most divinations). Some spells fit multiple categories, such as *teleport*.

**Level-Appropriate**: Compare the new spell to other spells in the same category at or near the desired spell level. Pay close attention to "must have" choices like *fireball*, *dimension door*, and *wall of force*. If the spell is more powerful or more useful than other spells of the desired level, increase the level. If it seems weak, consider lowering it. If a similar spell already exists, pay particular attention to relative power.

**Saving Throw or Attack Roll**: Most spells usable against others should require either a saving throw or an attack roll (generally touch or ranged touch). Spells quite powerful for their level, like *disintegrate* or *phantasmal killer*, may require both, or allow two saving throws. Watch out for spells that effectively take the target out of the fight and are negated by a saving throw; consider adding a minor effect even on a successful save, and toning down the result of a failed save. Spells that automatically affect the target should be higher level or limited in their consequences.

**Components**: Unless there is a good reason, almost all spells should require both verbal and somatic components, and most divine spells should require a divine focus. Spells with no verbal component are particularly rare. If the spell as designed lacks components, consider adding material component or focus requirements for flavor. Expensive components and foci are a good way to adjust the effective power of a spell without changing its level.

### Good Spells and Bad Spells

The best spells do something interesting even when the casting is not fully successful. They should have fairly simple mechanics without many ambiguities, special cases, or qualifications. If a spell takes a half-page or more to describe, it is probably too complicated.

Watch out for spells that counter or otherwise render useless equal- or higher-level magics. For defensive spells, countering an equal-level spell is fine (like *shield* negating *magic missile*), but an offensive spell generally should only overcome lower-level defenses or higher-level spells that duplicate those defenses (like *disintegrate* destroying both *wall of force* and *forcecage*).

Good spells expand upon the existing themes of magic in a novel manner. Watch for spells that break the implied limits of the game: most arcane casters have poor healing, and divine spells rarely excel at direct damage. With rare exception, spells should not duplicate existing class features or feats. Specialized spells (a brewer's blessing, a charm to hold a shoe on a horse) are a bad idea as a general rule, but if a player is excited about one, consider approving it even if it has little in-game application.

### Creating a Spell

Successfully researching a new spell requires time and expensive research. The research should cost at least 1,000 gp per spell level (more for particularly exotic spells) and require both Spellcraft and a Knowledge skill appropriate to the researcher's class. Wizards and bards use Knowledge (arcana); sorcerers use a Knowledge skill appropriate to their heritage (usually arcana, nature, or planes); druids and rangers use Knowledge (nature); clerics and paladins use Knowledge (religion).

For each week of research, the caster makes separate Knowledge and Spellcraft checks against a DC of 20 + twice the level of the spell being researched, modified according to the table below. To succeed, the caster must succeed at both checks. Failure indicates the week was wasted. Spells of 4th–6th level require 2 weeks of successful research, and spells of 7th–9th level require 4 weeks. The researcher may employ up to two assistants, who use the aid another action on the skill checks.

| Condition | DC Modifier |
|---|---|
| Caster already knows a similar spell | –2 |
| Per material component required | –2 (maximum –6) |
| Focus required | –2 to –5, based on cost and rarity |
| No verbal component | +10 |
| No somatic component | +5 |
| Additional research materials | –1 per 100 gp per spell level (maximum +5) |

## Spell Components

Most spell components are handled abstractly: they are purchased as part of a spell component pouch, which is assumed to contain the required quantity of any components for any spell the caster knows. For GMs wanting more verisimilitude, PCs can instead track down or prepare individual components, and even obtain enhanced components. Even then, most reagents are so common they can be assumed easily found. Save the legwork for commissioning tiny silver whistles (for *mage's faithful hound*) or finding dragon scales (for *form of the dragon*), and do not go overboard.

### Components for New Spells

Two principles of magic are **contagion** (a part retains a connection to the whole) and **sympathy** (like produces like). Most spell components are based on sympathy, either duplicating the desired outcome of the spell (gauze and a wisp of smoke for *gaseous form*) or having properties related to the spell (an owl's feather for *owl's wisdom*). When adding material components or a focus to a new spell, consider what forces the spell is shaping and select components that reflect them. Components need not be literally appropriate; the handful of earth for *detect undead* is a symbolic fit.

### Enhanced Components

An enhanced component is a specialized reagent used to improve a spell. The exact effects are up to the GM, but may include a small increase to save DC, caster level, damage, range, or duration. Particularly rare ones may even provide the benefits of metamagic feats. Enhanced components used in long-duration spells often make them significantly harder to dispel.

The power of a component is strongly influenced by how it was obtained. For most magic, the most powerful components are those freely given rather than taken by force, thievery, or magical compulsion. For darker magics, components obtained in profane and blasphemous rituals are the most powerful by far. Symbolism is key: a wizard seeking the last light of an eclipse to enhance *sunburst* might catch the sun's rays in a mirror.

Before introducing enhanced components, consider that they let casters increase their abilities without metamagic feats or permanent magic items. To preserve their rare and exotic feel, they should not be easily obtainable or fixed in price. Finding a source should be an adventure in itself, and quantities should be limited to a few castings.

- **Dragon's Tears**: These yellowish drops extend the duration of any spell that influences emotion, such as *heroism* or *rage*. If the tears are both genuine and freely given, the duration is increased by 50%. If obtained by trickery or magic, the increase is only 20%.
- **Vampire Dust**: The carefully sifted dust of a destroyed vampire enhances spells fueled by negative energy. A single vampire yields only 1d4+1 uses of dust. Spells infused with vampire dust receive a +1 bonus to caster level. If the caster personally slew the vampire, the dust increases save DCs by +1 as well.

## Wishes

*Wish* and *miracle* have the potential to drastically change a campaign. When the players reach 15th level and beyond, consider whether you want to allow them access to wishes, as soon enough they can cast the spell themselves. The easiest way to control *wish* is to restrict it to the options listed in the spell. Expanding the boundaries opens up roleplaying and story opportunities.

**Types of Wishes**: Treating all wishes the same is simple and keeps the game under control. A hierarchy gives story fodder: wishes from spells or magic items, followed by *miracle*, wishes granted by artifacts and relics, wishes granted by powerful outsiders like efreet and djinn, and finally wishes bestowed directly by gods and other entities beyond mortal ken.

**Making Good Wishes**: The best wishes are short, unambiguous, related to matters immediately at hand, and aimed at a simple (if powerful) task, such as restoring a sundered item or revealing the identity of a thief.

**Making Bad Wishes**: Wishes born of greed or vengeance have a way of turning sour. Attempts to guard against mishap with a list of conditions and qualifiers are rarely successful, most often resulting in partial fulfillment. Wishes that stretch the limits of the power granting them are ill advised. If the wish is from a spell or item, failure or backlash is likely; if from an outside source, the granter may be angered and twist the wish or seek retribution.

**Twisting Wishes**: The wishes most likely to be perverted are those granted by hostile outsiders, wishes from cursed objects, and bad wishes. Evil outsiders are loath to grant wishes that do not serve evil ends. A wish for eternal life may leave the wisher imprisoned in a decrepit yet still undying body. Wishes are best turned awry by adhering closely to the letter of the wish but violating the spirit.

**Deferred Results**: Rather than denying a particularly powerful wish, such as for the throne of a kingdom, grant it over an extended period. The wish subtly reshapes reality through coincidence, good fortune, and the timely appearance of helpful NPCs. Success is not assured unless the PC takes advantage of the opportunities.

## Making Magic Items

Pricing a magic item is more art than science. The formulas in the magic item creation rules are often not applied exactly: the *ring of invisibility* has a calculated price of 10,800 gp but a book value of 20,000 gp, because its at-will nature offsets the biggest drawback of invisibility (it ends after attacking). Watch out for any item that counteracts a basic weakness of an ability, class, or spell.

The best test is to compare the new item to "must have" items (weapons, armor, stat-boosting gear), to other items that share the same slot, and to items with similar powers. As a rule of thumb, if you would take the item in a heartbeat over a more expensive standard item, it is probably too cheap; if you would never consider taking a 10,000 gp ring over a *belt of physical might +2* or a +2 battleaxe, it might be overpriced. It is safer to price items too high than too low.

**Ad-Hoc Pricing**: If nothing in the pricing table matches the new item's powers, look for something close among existing magic items. For example, a weapon ability that lets the wielder draw the weapon as a free action is essentially Quick Draw tied to one weapon. *Gloves of arrow snaring* are a 4,000 gp item that grants a feat, so use that as the base. Multiple abilities on an item normally increase the cost by 50%, but since the feat would allow drawing items other than a single weapon, the GM splits the difference and makes it a 5,000 gp weapon enhancement.

**Keeping the Balance**:

- **Disadvantages That Aren't**: Be wary of items designed with a class or alignment restriction in order to lower the price. If the restriction does not restrict the character who will use the item, it is not a drawback and should not reduce the price.
- **Slotless Items**: The game assumes a limit on how many magic items a character can use at once. Slotless items like ioun stones are usually relatively low-powered or specialized. Think carefully before allowing a new item that duplicates an old one without using up an item slot.
- **Good Item, Bad Formula**: Compare what the item does to the formula used and tweak the cost or powers to match the benefit. A ring that provides *mind blank* continuously calculates to 153,000 gp, while one that casts it once per day (24 hours per use) comes out to 55,080 gp. There is little difference in utility between the two, and the first is a better fit for the theme of continuous mental protection.

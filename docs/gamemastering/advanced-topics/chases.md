# Chases

While chases are a signature action scene in countless stories, they present a singular challenge, thanks to static movement rates. Since every creature in the game has a set movement rate, it might seem like you would either automatically (or never) catch up to a fleeing foe. There is more to catching a foe or avoiding being caught than simple speed.

## Building a Chase

To simulate a chase, you need a little prep work. Take about 10 small pieces of paper (playing-card or sticky-note size). These "chase cards" represent the chase's route, like the route a board game takes. Ten cards works well for a standard chase, but you can use more for a longer chase.

When laying out the "track," decide if there is a preset ending (a "finish line"), be it a contested resource, an escape vehicle, a portal that whisks away the pursued foe, or some similar goal that the fleeing character is trying to reach before he gets caught. If the chase has a finish line, mark one of the cards as such. If the pursuers haven't caught the fleeing character by the time he reaches this card, the chase ends. If the chase has no finish line and is merely a race of attrition, lay the cards out in a square, circle, or similar shape so that there is no obvious beginning or ending. You can even lay the cards out in a grid, allowing participants to move about a field of obstacles in any direction.

Pick two chase obstacles for each card. Not every card needs obstacles (there is no need to place obstacles on the finish line card, and for a faster chase you can place obstacles on fewer cards), but if a card has obstacles, it should have two choices.

When the chase begins, place miniatures or tokens representing the creatures on the cards according to the starting conditions:

- **Sudden Start**: Everyone begins at the same starting point; all participants start on the same card.
- **Head Start**: A participant with a head start begins three cards ahead of the rest.
- **Long Shot**: One participant is so far ahead that he has practically already won. He begins either three cards from the end of the chase or 10 cards away from the rest of the participants, whichever is the greater distance between the two sides.

## Chase Obstacles

Tailor the obstacles to the location. A rooftop chase might include crumbling rooftops, narrow gaps to leap across, tightropes, or steep roofs to clamber up. A chase through a crumbling swamp ruin might involve crumbling walkways, narrow passageways, grasping vines, leaps over quicksand, or clouds of nauseating miasma. Mix up the flavor of the obstacles as well as the types of checks and DCs needed to navigate them.

| Obstacle | DC |
|---|---|
| Trivial | 10 |
| Simple | 15 |
| Standard | 20 |
| Difficult | 25 |
| Very difficult | 30 |

For high-level chases, assign correspondingly high DCs. It is best to have the DCs of both obstacles on a card within 5 points of each other, but never identical; this forces participants to make tactical choices.

As a general rule, obstacles should be overcome by physical skill checks such as Acrobatics, Climb, Escape Artist, Ride, or Swim. Perception can be used for obstacles like shortcuts, Stealth for obstacles requiring someone to move through a square quietly, and Bluff to convince a city guard that you should be allowed to pass. You can even use saving throws (a Fortitude save to avoid being sickened by passing through a pool of filth, or a Will save to evade strange wailing spirits). It is fine to reuse obstacles, but vary them between cards.

## Running a Chase

First determine the baseline speed: the movement rate of the majority of participants. In most cases this is a land speed of 30 feet. This base speed sets the "distance" of each chase card, so in most cases each card represents 30 feet of space.

For some chases, such as two sailing ships racing to a distant island or a long overland journey, adjust the timing: decide how long each round of the chase takes and adjust the distance of each card as appropriate. This also gives participants multiple options each turn to do things like cast spells.

At the start of a chase, each participant makes an Initiative check to determine the order in which he moves. (If a participant triggers the chase with an initial action, such as a prisoner making a mad dash for freedom, that participant goes first in a surprise round if he successfully surprises the other creatures.)

A character's actual speed does not directly affect how often he moves between cards, but it affects how quickly he navigates obstacles. For each 10 feet slower than the baseline speed a character moves, he suffers a cumulative –2 penalty on any check made to navigate obstacles. For every 10 feet faster than the baseline speed, he gains a cumulative +2 bonus. Significant mobility advantages over the baseline speed type (such as flight) grant an additional +10 bonus on checks made to avoid obstacles. Extremely powerful effects (such as teleportation) allow a character to instantly move forward a number of cards (use each card's distance to determine ranges).

Using 30-foot cards, it takes a move action to move through a single card. When a character exits a card, he must choose one of that card's two obstacles and face it as a standard action before moving to the next card. Success means the character moves to the next card; failure means he must face the obstacle again next round. Instead of exiting a card, a character can take another action not directly related to navigating the course, such as casting a spell or drawing a weapon.

A character who wants to move three cards during his turn can do so by taking a full-round action. He must overcome both obstacles on the card he is leaving. If he fails either obstacle check by 5 or less, he moves only one card forward and his turn ends. If he fails either check by more than 5, he cannot move at all that turn. A character who fails two obstacle checks in a turn becomes **mired** in his current square (he might have fallen from a ledge, gotten a foot stuck amid roots, or been caught in a crowd). A mired character must spend another full-round action becoming unmired and effectively loses his next turn in the chase. In some cases, becoming mired might impart additional penalties (such as falling damage).

A character can also make a ranged attack or cast a spell during his turn in a chase. If the action is a full-round action, he cannot move at all. Use the number of cards and their established distances to determine ranges. The terrain might provide the target partial or even full cover or concealment. A character can make melee attacks only against targets on the same card.
